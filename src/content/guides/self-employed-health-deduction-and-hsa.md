Fewer people are holding on to their own coverage. **KFF Health News** reported on September 15, in [Rising Healthcare Costs Have Hit Georgia's Most Conservative District. But Its Politics Are Unlikely To Change.](https://kffhealthnews.org/health-care-costs/high-healthcare-costs-georgia-14th-trump-aca-obamacare-marjorie-taylor-greene/), that "with the enhanced subsidies expiring at the end of 2025, ACA enrollment fell by nearly 3 million this year."

People with a job-based plan are feeling it too, but they have help. **CBS News** reported on September 4 that [Employer and worker health plan costs expected to jump in 2027](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/employer-health-care-costs-increase-2027/), and Mary Cunningham notes that "employers typically absorb only around 80% of health plan costs, with workers shouldering the rest."

If you work for yourself, nobody absorbs 80% of anything. That matters for a lot of people: KFF estimates 48% of adults under 65 with individual market coverage are self-employed, small-business owners or small-business employees.<sup><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup> And insurers proposed a median 15% marketplace premium increase for 2027.<sup><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup> The tax code does give you two tools, though. ## Break 1: the self-employed health insurance deduction

If you were self-employed and had a net profit for the year on Schedule C or Schedule F, you can generally deduct the health insurance premiums you paid for yourself, your spouse and your dependents. It also covers a child who was under 27 at the end of the year.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> You figure it on Form 7206, and it goes on Schedule 1, so you get it even if you take the standard deduction.

The rules that trip people up:

- **It's month by month.** You can't take it for any month you were eligible for an employer-subsidized plan, including your spouse's, even if you didn't sign up.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> A spouse with a job-based plan available all year usually means no deduction.
- **You need the profit.** The deduction can't be more than your net earnings from the business the plan is set up under. A year with a loss means no deduction.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>
- **It doesn't cut self-employment tax.** The IRS says you can't subtract it when you figure net earnings for self-employment tax.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> It lowers your income tax only.
- **Subsidized dollars don't count.** You can't deduct the part of your premium that your premium tax credit paid.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> Because the credit and the deduction each depend on the other, the IRS gives a step-by-step method for figuring both.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> Tax software usually handles it, but it's a good reason to ask a tax pro the first year.

Medicare premiums you pay in your own name and qualified long-term care premiums can count too, which is worth knowing if you're still working for yourself past 65.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

## Break 2: an HSA with a bronze plan

Starting with 2026, the IRS treats bronze and catastrophic plans as HSA-eligible high-deductible plans.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> That's new, and it's a big deal for people who buy their own coverage, since bronze is where many shoppers land when premiums climb.

For 2027, you can contribute up to $4,500 with self-only coverage or $9,000 with family coverage.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> If you're 55 or older, you can add $1,000.<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup> You deduct contributions even if you don't itemize, and you report them on Form 8889 and Schedule 1, not on Schedule C.<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup> Money you spend from the account on qualified medical costs comes out tax-free.

The same IRS notice lets a direct primary care membership sit alongside an HSA. The fees can't top $150 a month for one person or $300 for a family, and they count as medical expenses you can pay from the account.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> A DPC membership covers only that practice's own clinicians, so unless your current doctor is part of it, joining means switching. For more, see [DPC and your HSA in 2026](/blog/dpc-and-your-hsa-2026/).

## A worked example

Meet Dana, a hypothetical freelance designer with no employer plan available to her or through a spouse. Her 2027 numbers are round on purpose:

- Net profit: $90,000, which puts her over the subsidy cliff, so she gets no premium tax credit
- Bronze premium: $500 a month, or $6,000 for the year
- HSA contribution: $4,500
- DPC membership: $75 a month, or $900, paid from the HSA

If her top federal income tax rate is 22%, here's roughly what the two breaks are worth in federal income tax:

- Premium deduction: $6,000 × 22% = $1,320
- HSA deduction: $4,500 × 22% = $990
- Total: about $2,310

Her self-employment tax doesn't change, and state taxes vary. Her $6,000 premium costs her closer to $4,680 after the deduction, and her DPC fees come out of money that was never taxed. If Dana did get a premium tax credit, only the part of the premium she paid herself would be deductible, and the numbers would shift.

## Questions to ask before you pick a plan

- **Could I have joined an employer plan in any month, mine or my spouse's?** Those months are out.
- **Will my business show a profit this year?** No profit, no premium deduction.
- **Is the plan bronze or catastrophic, or does it meet the HDHP rules?** That decides whether you can open an HSA.
- **Will I get a premium tax credit?** If so, ask your tax pro how the credit and deduction work together.
- **Does my DPC fee stay under $150 a month ($300 for a family)?**
