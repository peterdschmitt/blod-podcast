For years, the pitch from the biggest health companies was that owning everything would make care simpler. This week one of them changed course. **The New York Times**'s Rebecca Robbins reported on November 4 that [CVS to split off pharmacy business, ending the healthcare conglomerate era](https://www.inquirer.com/business/health/cvs-pharmacy-business-split-20261104.html). She noted the criticism behind it: "Critics complain that the conglomerates use their size and leverage to steer patients toward their own pharmacies."

**Bloomberg**'s John Tozzi laid out who goes where in [CVS Plans to Break Up $117 Billion Healthcare Conglomerate](https://www.newstribune.com/news/2026/nov/05/cvs-plans-to-break-up-117-billion-health-care/). The drugstores become a new company called CVS Pharmacy in the first half of 2028, and "Minute Clinics and the mail-order pharmacy would stay with the retail entity."

That's a big change for a company that touches tens of millions of people's coverage. Here's what it does and doesn't mean for you.

## Who ends up where

After the split, the company called CVS Health keeps Aetna, the Caremark pharmacy benefit business, its specialty pharmacies, and the Oak Street Health and Signify Health care businesses. CVS Pharmacy gets the stores, MinuteClinic and mail-order pharmacy. As of September 30, CVS reported about 9,000 retail pharmacies, more than 1,000 walk-in and primary care clinics, about 87 million pharmacy benefit members and an estimated 37 million people in its health insurance products.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

## What changes for you right now

Nothing, according to the company. CVS says, "Customers, patients and clients will continue to receive their medicine, care, benefits and services, uninterrupted."<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> Your Aetna card, your Caremark prescription coverage and your local store keep working as they do today.

That's a promise about today, not a forecast for 2028. How prices, pharmacy networks or plan designs might change after the split isn't known yet, and we won't guess.

## Why this matters beyond CVS

Many people don't realize their coverage involves three separate jobs. Your **insurer** decides what's covered. Your **pharmacy benefit manager** runs your drug coverage, including which drugs are on the list and which pharmacies are in network. Your **pharmacy** fills the prescription. At CVS, one company has owned all three.

The FTC found that the top three pharmacy benefit managers processed nearly 80% of about 6.6 billion prescriptions, and warned they "may be steering patients to their affiliated pharmacies."<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> The Times reports that Arkansas and Tennessee have passed laws aimed at stopping companies from both managing prescription benefits and running pharmacies.

## What to do

- **If you're on Medicare:** CVS Health holds about 12% of Medicare Advantage enrollment.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> The split alone isn't a reason to switch. But you can change plans through December 7, with changes starting January 1.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> Compare on your doctors, your drugs and your total costs.
- **If you have Caremark drug coverage through work:** Check your 2027 formulary and pharmacy list during open enrollment, as you would any year.
- **Know who to call.** A coverage question goes to your insurer. A drug coverage or pharmacy network question goes to your pharmacy benefit manager. The number is usually on your member card.
- **Watch the extras.** Retail clinic visits and other value-added services may sit with a different company after 2028. See [Value-added benefits, explained: what is worth paying for](/blog/value-added-benefits-explained/).

If you'd like everyday care that doesn't depend on any of these companies, a direct primary care membership is one option. It isn't insurance, and it covers visits only with that practice's own clinicians. See [Direct primary care, explained honestly](/blog/direct-primary-care-explained/).
