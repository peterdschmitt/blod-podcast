When every bill looks bigger, a flat monthly fee for your doctor starts to sound good. That is the pitch of direct primary care. It is a real option for everyday care, but only if you know exactly where it stops.

## What the membership usually covers

You pay the practice a set fee each month, about $98.64 on average nationally, and it does not bill insurance for the care the fee covers.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> Every contract is different, but most pure DPC practices include:

- Unlimited office visits, often same day or next day
- Texting, phone and video visits with the practice's clinicians
- Annual physicals and ongoing care for conditions like high blood pressure or diabetes
- Basic in-office procedures, such as stitches or a joint injection
- Labs and generic drugs at close to the practice's cost, where state law allows

The American Academy of Family Physicians says the model cuts the overhead of billing insurers and gives doctors more time with each patient.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

One condition comes with all of it. The membership covers the practice's own clinicians, not any doctor you choose. Unless your current doctor is part of the practice or program, joining usually means switching doctors. Our [DPC explainer](/blog/direct-primary-care-explained/) covers how that works.

## The five things it never covers

ValuePenguin puts it plainly: a DPC membership won't cover specialists, emergency room care or hospital costs, and you should keep regular health insurance alongside it.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> In practice, five big categories fall outside the fee.

1. **The ER and the ambulance.** If you need emergency care, the hospital bills you or your insurer, not your DPC practice.
2. **Hospital stays and surgery.** This includes having a baby. HealthCare.gov puts the average 3-day hospital stay at around $30,000.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>
3. **Specialists.** Your DPC doctor can refer you to a cardiologist or an orthopedist, and many DPC practices have networks they can refer you into.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> The specialist still bills you separately.
4. **Most imaging.** MRIs and CT scans happen outside the practice. Some practices have arranged cash prices with imaging centers, so ask.
5. **Brand-name and specialty drugs.** A practice may dispense generics at cost. Brand-name and specialty prescriptions go through a pharmacy, and through your insurance if you have it.

None of these is a flaw in the model. It is the design. The flat fee works because the practice only pays for its own care. The big, rare bills are the job of an insurance plan.

## One year, two bad days

Take Maya, a hypothetical 42-year-old who pays $95 a month for DPC. That is $1,140 for the year. She sees her DPC doctor five times, gets two lab panels and refills a generic blood pressure drug. All of that runs through the membership, with the labs and the drug at the practice's low prices.

Then she falls on the ice and breaks her leg. HealthCare.gov says fixing a broken leg can cost up to $7,500.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> Her DPC practice can help her find an orthopedist. It pays none of the bill.

Here is how the year looks with DPC alone and with DPC next to a Marketplace plan. For 2027, no Marketplace plan can make one person pay more than $12,000 out of pocket for covered in-network care, and many plans set a lower limit.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup>

Be honest about the middle row. With a high-deductible plan, Maya may pay most of a $7,500 broken leg herself, since the deductible comes first.<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup> What insurance changes is the bottom row. Without it there is no ceiling. With it, a very bad year has one.

The plan also costs premiums every month, which this table leaves out. Our [DPC plus a bronze plan](/blog/dpc-plus-bronze-worked-numbers/) post runs a full year with premiums, an HSA and both a quiet year and a rough one.

## Questions to ask before you sign

The contract is where the line gets drawn for your practice. Before you join, ask:

- **What exactly is included?** Get the list of visits, procedures and services in writing.
- **What do labs and generic drugs cost?** Ask for a few sample prices.
- **How do referrals work?** Ask whether the practice helps you find specialists and imaging at a known price.
- **Who will I see?** Ask which clinicians are part of the membership, since those are the only ones it covers.
- **How do I cancel?** Look for any enrollment fee and the notice you have to give.
