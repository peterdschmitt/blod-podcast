## How to use this checklist

Pick two or three practices near you and call or visit each. Most offer a free meet-and-greet. Ask every practice the same questions, write the answers down, then compare. A good practice will be glad you asked.

To find practices, try the DPC Frontier mapper<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> or the DPC Alliance physician directory,<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup> or search "direct primary care" plus your town. Both directories are run by groups that support the model, so treat a listing as a starting point, not an endorsement. We don't rank or recommend specific practices.

## 1. Price and contract

1. **What is the monthly fee for me, and for each family member?** About three in four practices (76%) set prices by age.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> Get the total for your whole household.
2. **Is there an enrollment or annual fee?** Some practices charge a one-time fee. That's fine if they tell you up front.
3. **How long is the commitment, and how do I cancel?** Month-to-month with 30 days' notice is common. Be wary of a 12-month lock-in with a penalty.
4. **Can the fee go up, and how much notice will I get?**
5. **Can I read the membership agreement before I pay?** If they say no, you have your answer.

## 2. What is included

6. **Which visits, procedures and tests does the fee cover?** Ask for the list in writing.
7. **What costs extra, and how much?** Labs, generic drugs, imaging and procedures should have posted prices.
8. **Do you dispense medications in the office?** Not every practice can. State rules on doctors dispensing drugs differ a lot, and a few states sharply limit it.<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup> If they do dispense, ask the price of each drug you take now.
9. **Do you have cash prices for imaging, specialists or surgery centers?** Many practices have arranged these. For someone paying out of pocket, the savings can run to hundreds of dollars.

## 3. Access

10. **How many patients does each doctor have, and what is the cap?** In the DPC Alliance survey, doctors who considered their practices full mostly reported 400 to 700 patients.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> Ask how close to full this one is.
11. **How soon can I get a sick visit?** Same day or next day is the norm.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>
12. **Can I text or call the doctor directly? Who answers nights and weekends?**
13. **Who covers when the doctor is on vacation?**
14. **Will you work with my specialists and hospital,** sending records and making referrals?

## 4. Money rules: HSA and Medicare

15. **Do you meet the IRS definition of a direct primary care service arrangement?** For you to stay HSA-eligible, the fixed monthly fee can't top $150 for one person or $300 for a family, and it has to cover primary care only.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> Ask the practice to put its answer in writing.
16. **Do you bill insurance for anything?** Under the IRS rules, the flat fee must be the practice's only pay for the care it covers. A practice that also bills insurance for those services doesn't qualify.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> A hybrid practice can still give good care, but it changes the HSA answer. See [DPC and your HSA](/blog/dpc-and-your-hsa-2026/).
17. **Have you opted out of Medicare?** About 81% of DPC doctors in the survey have.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> If yours has, Medicare won't pay for anything you get from that doctor, even covered services, except in an emergency. You would sign a private contract and pay the doctor yourself.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> If you are on Medicare or close to it, call your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) before you sign. Its counselors are free and don't sell anything.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>

## 5. Quality and fit

18. **Who will I see, and what is their training?** Your membership only pays for care from this practice's own clinicians. See anyone else and you pay full price unless a separate health plan covers it.<sup><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup> So get names, not just "our providers." If the program books visits for you across a network of partner clinics, ask which clinics are near you and whether you can ask for the same clinician each time. Board certification in family or internal medicine is a good sign. You can check a doctor's license and any disciplinary record on your state medical board's website or through the free DocInfo lookup.<sup><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup>
19. **Is my current doctor part of this?** If not, joining means switching primary care doctors. Weigh that honestly. For some people a long relationship with a doctor is worth more than the flat fee.
20. **How long has the practice been open, and how many members has it lost?** Stability matters. If a practice closes, you start the search over.
21. **What do you tell members about insurance?** The right answer: membership covers primary care only, and you still need a health plan for emergencies, hospital stays and specialists.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>

## In the news

Membership medicine is getting more attention from national reporters. Two recent stories worth reading:

- **KFF Health News**, Apr 16, 2025: [In Rural Massachusetts, Patients and Physicians Weigh Trade-Offs of Concierge Medicine](https://kffhealthnews.org/health-care-costs/concierge-medicine-direct-primary-care-doctor-shortage-rural-western-massachusetts/). When a doctor caps a practice, the patients left out feel it, which is one reason to ask about panel size and how stable the practice is.
