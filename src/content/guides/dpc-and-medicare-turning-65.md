Medicare is back in the headlines as its fall shopping season nears. On October 8, **Reuters** reported that [Most Medicare Advantage drug plan enrollees in high-quality plans, US government says](https://wsau.com/?p=1024445). About 71% of people in those plans are in contracts projected to earn four stars or more for 2027, and higher ratings bring insurers bonus payments that add up to billions of dollars.

The same day, **KFF Health News** published ['Medicare for All' Is Back. Now Comes the Hard Part.](https://kffhealthnews.org/health-care-costs/medicare-for-all-proposals-universal-healthcare-implementation-difficult/) Elisabeth Rosenthal reports that more than half of physicians have said they'd prefer some sort of single-payer system.

Whatever happens in Washington, one question is personal if you have a direct primary care membership and a 65th birthday coming up: what happens to your doctor when Medicare starts? The answer depends on one detail most members never think to ask about.

## Your Medicare clock

Your first chance to sign up is the Initial Enrollment Period. It lasts seven months, starting three months before the month you turn 65 and ending three months after it.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> If you miss it and don't have other qualifying coverage, you may wait to sign up and pay a Part B late penalty for as long as you have Part B.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

Once you're on Medicare, you can change plans each year during open enrollment, October 15 to December 7.<sup><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup>

## The question to ask your DPC practice

Ask one thing: **"Have you opted out of Medicare?"** The answer sorts every DPC practice into one of two groups.

**If the doctor opted out.** Many DPC doctors don't bill any insurance, and for Medicare that usually means opting out.<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup> To keep seeing you, the doctor has you sign a private contract before providing care. In it, you agree to pay the doctor's charges yourself and not to send a claim to Medicare.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> Medicare then pays nothing for any service from that doctor, even one Medicare normally covers.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> That holds inside a Medicare Advantage plan too.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> The one exception is an emergency or urgent situation.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup><sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> Opt-outs last at least two years and renew automatically.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup><sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>

**If the doctor stayed in Medicare.** The practice bills Medicare for covered visits, the way any other doctor's office does. What it can charge you on top is narrower. Federal health officials have warned doctors that charging extra fees for services Medicare already covers can bring fines and exclusion from Medicare, while contracts for services Medicare truly doesn't cover are allowed.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> So a membership at such a practice should pay only for extras Medicare doesn't. Ask the practice to show you what the fee buys.

Either way, the membership doesn't replace Medicare. You still need Medicare for hospital stays and specialists, and a drug plan for prescriptions, which a DPC membership doesn't cover.<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup>

## What it costs at 65

DPC fees often rise with age. A 2023 roundup found practices charging patients 65 and older $110 to $150 a month.<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup> That comes on top of your Medicare costs. For 2027, the Part B premium is forecast to rise to $209.50 a month, though CMS won't confirm it until November.<sup><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup>

Take Carol, a hypothetical 64-year-old who pays $120 a month for DPC and turns 65 next spring. Her doctor has opted out.

- DPC membership: $120 × 12 = $1,440 a year
- Part B at the forecast premium: $209.50 × 12 = $2,514 a year
- Together: $3,954 a year, before any Part D drug plan, Medigap policy or Medicare Advantage premium

Medicare covers none of the $1,440. Whether the membership is worth it depends on how often Carol sees her doctor and how much she values same-day access. If she keeps it, her other Medicare coverage still handles the big bills. Once she joins Medicare, every primary care visit with this doctor is on her, so she'd want to know where she'd go if the practice ever closes.

## What happens to your HSA

Starting with the first month you're enrolled in Medicare, you can't put any more money into an HSA. That applies to retroactive Medicare coverage too, so contributions made during a backdated period count as excess.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> If your Medicare start may be backdated, check with a tax professional before you make a final contribution.

The money already in the account is still yours to spend. After 65, HSA funds can pay Medicare premiums, though not Medigap premiums.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> IRS guidance treats DPC fees as medical expenses an HSA can reimburse.<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup> For the full rules on fees and caps, see [DPC and your HSA in 2026](/blog/dpc-and-your-hsa-2026/).

## If you're choosing a DPC practice near 65

- **Ask about Medicare first.** Opted out, or still billing Medicare? Get the answer in writing.
- **Ask what the fee covers at your age.** Prices often step up at 65.
- **Ask what happens in an emergency.** The opt-out rules have a narrow exception for emergencies and urgent care.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>
- **Know you may be switching doctors.** A membership covers only that practice's own clinicians, so unless your current doctor is part of it, joining means a new doctor.<sup><a href="#src-10">10</a></sup>

For what a membership does and doesn't include, read [What a DPC membership covers, and five things it never will](/blog/what-dpc-covers-and-five-things-it-never-will/).
