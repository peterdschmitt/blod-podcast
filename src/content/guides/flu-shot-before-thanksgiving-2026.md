Flu didn't wait for the holidays this year. **AP**'s Mike Stobbe reported in [The US flu season is off to a very early start. It's hitting the South and West now](https://www.wsbtv.com/news/health/us-flu-season-is-off/FBO2NEZGV44PJFX4LGXGUDTJCA/) that "The U.S. flu season is off to a very early start, and it's quickly intensifying." Dr. Fred Lopez of LSU Health Sciences Center put it plainly: "If you haven't been vaccinated, you should get vaccinated now."

The same story is playing out state by state. **CBS News**' Minnesota station asked [Why is the flu season ramping up early?](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/flu-season-ramping-up-early-explainer/) Jeff Wagner wrote that "This year's flu season isn't waiting for the holidays to hit hard," and that "The latest numbers from the Minnesota Department of Health show 166 hospitalizations from the virus so far this season." That compares with about 10 at this point in each of the past two years, he reported.

With Thanksgiving next week, here's how to get protected in time, and how to make sure the shot doesn't cost you anything.

## How early, and how big

CDC's latest weekly report, released Nov 20, puts 2 jurisdictions at very high flu activity and 9 more at high. It estimates at least 3.7 million illnesses, 50,000 hospitalizations and 2,800 deaths so far this season, and it has recorded seven flu deaths in children. Of the flu A viruses CDC subtyped that week, 96.4% were H1N1.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

CDC recommends a flu shot every season for nearly everyone: "Everyone 6 months and older should get a flu vaccine every season with rare exceptions."<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

## Is it too late?

No. "It takes about two weeks after vaccination for antibodies that protect against flu to develop in the body," CDC says.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> Vanderbilt's Dr. William Schaffner told NPR the shot reaches full strength in about ten days, so there's still time before Thanksgiving. A shot this weekend gets you most of the way there for the holiday and fully covered for December gatherings.

## When it's $0

- **ACA-compliant plans, including most job-based plans:** you generally won't pay a copay or coinsurance for "immunizations and screening tests," "even if you haven't met your deductible." That applies when the shot is given by an in-network provider. HealthCare.gov adds that "$0 cost isn't guaranteed in all cases," so check that the pharmacy is in your network.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>
- **Medicare:** Part B covers the seasonal flu shot. "You pay nothing for a flu vaccine if your doctor or other health care provider accepts assignment for giving the vaccine."<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>
- **No coverage, or out of network:** prices vary by pharmacy and by the type of shot. Ask for the price before you get it, and check your local health department for low-cost clinics.

## Where direct primary care fits

A direct primary care doctor can tell you which flu shot fits your age and health, and whether to pair it with other shots this season. Ask whether the practice stocks flu vaccine or sends members to a pharmacy. If it's a pharmacy, use your insurance there so the shot counts as covered preventive care. If you do get sick, a membership practice may be able to see you or talk with you quickly, which matters because flu treatment works best when it starts early. A DPC membership isn't insurance, and it covers only the practice's own clinicians. Our [plain-English guide to direct primary care](/blog/direct-primary-care-explained/) walks through how it works.

## What to do this weekend

- **Get the shot now** if you haven't, especially if you're traveling or seeing older relatives or young kids.
- **Use an in-network pharmacy or office,** and bring your insurance card.
- **On Medicare, confirm the provider accepts assignment** so you pay nothing.
- **Have a plan if you get sick.** Know how you'll reach your doctor quickly over the holiday.
