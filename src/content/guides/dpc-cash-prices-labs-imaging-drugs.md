Drug prices are still a moving target. The **AP** reported on September 18 that [Trump announces all 50 states joining Medicaid drug pricing model](https://www.wbtv.com/2026/09/18/trump-announces-all-50-states-joining-medicaid-drug-pricing-model/). Fatima Hussein writes that the White House says its drugmaker deals could save $529 billion over 10 years, but with the terms kept private, the savings are hard to verify independently.

Meanwhile, plenty of people are paying up front just to get care. **KFF Health News** reported on October 15, in [What Texas Reveals About the Obstacles to Getting Hospital Financial Aid](https://kffhealthnews.org/health-industry/hospital-financial-aid-obstacles-presumptive-eligibility-texas-oregon/), that one patient, Aimee Carlock, borrowed money to prepay $5,500 so she could schedule uterine surgery.

That's the backdrop for one of direct primary care's least-known features: many practices sell the everyday extras, like lab tests, generic drugs and scans, at prices that can look like typos. Here's where those prices come from and when they help.

## What DPC practices sell at cash prices

A DPC membership covers your visits with the practice. Labs, drugs and imaging are often extra, but many practices pass along wholesale prices for them.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> AAFP notes DPC practices may be able to negotiate cash discounts with large lab chains and local imaging centers.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

Here's what two practices post, read on October 16 and 17, 2026. Prices change, so treat these as examples.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup><sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>

Elevated Health also lists generics, with the retail price beside its own: lisinopril 20mg at $0.99 vs. $4.00, metformin 1000mg at $2.40 vs. $4.00, and doxycycline 100mg at $3.36 vs. $80.00. For imaging, it lists member prices of $65 for an X-ray, $200 to $300 for a CT scan and $300 to $400 for most MRIs.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> Mira says, "You pay exactly what the lab charges us."<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>

## Why cash can be so cheap

Part of it is that the practice isn't billing an insurer, so there's no claim to process and no negotiated rate in the middle. Many practices buy tests and drugs at wholesale and pass that price along, and some, like Mira, say they add nothing on top.

The other part is how much insured prices vary. Large employer health plans paid an average of $861 for an MRI of the lower spine in 2018, with metro-area averages from $400 to $1,100.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> Even at hospitals, cash can win. A 2023 Health Affairs study found hospital cash prices were lower than the median commercial rate in 47% of cases.<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup> Hospitals now have to post their discounted cash prices, along with at least 300 "shoppable services," so you can check.<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup>

## The catch: your deductible

When you pay cash outside your insurance, that money usually doesn't count toward your deductible or out-of-pocket maximum. NBC News makes the same point about TrumpRx.

For small, routine costs, that rarely matters. A year of four basic labs and two generics at Elevated Health's prices comes to about $56. Here's the math:

- Labs: $4.46 + $5.25 + $2.24 + $2.97 = $14.92
- Two generics: ($0.99 + $2.40) × 12 months = $40.68
- Total: $55.60

It matters more for a big item like an MRI. If you expect to hit your deductible this year anyway, running the scan through insurance may bring you closer to the point where your plan starts paying more. If you don't, the cash price may simply be the cheaper bill. You can ask your insurer whether it will count a cash purchase toward your deductible.

## Paying with an HSA

Lab fees, prescribed medicines and X-rays for medical reasons all count as medical expenses under IRS rules,<sup><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup> so you can generally pay these cash prices from an HSA. Keep the receipts. One limit applies to the membership itself: to work with an HSA, the DPC fee can't include prescription drugs, other than vaccines.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> Buying drugs from the practice separately is the usual setup.

## Where you live matters

Not every state lets doctors sell drugs in the office. Massachusetts prohibits most physician dispensing, New Hampshire limits doctors to a three-day supply for immediate needs, and New Jersey caps it at a seven-day supply.<sup><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup> In those states, a practice may send you to a pharmacy or a low-cost online option instead.

A membership also covers only that practice's own clinicians, so unless your current doctor is part of it, joining means switching.<sup><a href="#src-10">10</a></sup>

## Questions to ask a practice

- **Do you post prices for labs, generics and imaging, and are they member-only?**
- **Do you dispense drugs in the office, or send prescriptions to a pharmacy?**
- **Which imaging centers do you work with, and what do common scans cost?**
- **Can you give me an itemized receipt for my HSA?**

For what a membership does and doesn't cover, see [What a DPC membership covers, and five things it never will](/blog/what-dpc-covers-and-five-things-it-never-will/).
