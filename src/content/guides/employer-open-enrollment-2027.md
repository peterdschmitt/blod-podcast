The price jump isn't only hitting people who buy their own coverage. **KFF Health News** reported on October 22 that [Job-Based Health Plan Costs Rise, With Little Relief in Sight](https://kffhealthnews.org/insurance/kff-survey-employer-based-health-plan-costs-rise-2026-2027/). Julie Appleby writes that the average family premium rose 5% to $28,303 this year, and that consultants project employer costs will climb another 9.5% (Aon) or 8.2% (Marsh) in 2027.

Some workers are pushing back. The **AP**'s Sophie Austin reported on October 21 that [California state workers begin 1-day strike over return-to-office mandate, pay and healthcare](https://www.ksat.com/news/national/2026/10/21/california-state-workers-begin-1-day-strike-over-return-to-office-mandate-pay-and-healthcare/). Their union wants the state to "increase the state contribution toward employees' healthcare costs."

Most of us won't be on a picket line. We'll be in a benefits portal this month, deciding which plan to pick. Here's how to make that choice with clear eyes.

## What changed this year

KFF's 2026 survey of employers found average annual premiums of $9,804 for single coverage and $28,303 for family coverage. Workers paid $1,672 and $7,471 of that, on average.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> CNN notes that workers' raises averaged only 3.7%, according to KFF, so for many households the premium is growing faster than the paycheck.

Deductibles keep climbing too. Among covered workers with a general annual deductible, the average is $1,955, and 38% have a deductible of $2,000 or more for single coverage.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

## Don't just roll over

Many employers let you keep last year's choice by doing nothing. That's convenient, but 2027 plans may not match 2026 ones. Before your deadline:

- **Compare the full cost, not just the paycheck deduction.** Add a year of premiums to the deductible and the out-of-pocket maximum to see your worst-case year for each plan.
- **Check your doctors.** CNN reports some workers may get lower copays by seeing doctors in newly designated preferred networks.
- **Check your prescriptions.** Look up each one on the plan's drug list, and make sure your out-of-pocket cost hasn't gone up.
- **Look at the spouse's plan, too.** If two of you have job coverage, compare both offers, including what each charges to add family members.
- **Know your deadline.** Job-based plans set their own enrollment periods,<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> and many run only a few weeks.

## If you're choosing an HSA plan

About 29% of covered workers are now in an HSA-qualified plan.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> For 2027, the IRS says those plans need a deductible of at least $1,750 for self-only coverage or $3,500 for family coverage, with out-of-pocket costs capped at $8,700 and $17,400. You can contribute up to $4,500 (self-only) or $9,000 (family) to an HSA.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

An HSA plan can make sense if you can afford the deductible and your employer adds money to the account. Only a small share of HSA plan enrollees get an employer contribution that covers their whole deductible, so check what yours offers.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

If you have, or are thinking about, a direct primary care membership, the IRS's 2027 figures matter here. A DPC arrangement doesn't stop you from contributing to an HSA as long as its fees total no more than $150 a month, or $300 if it covers more than one person.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> DPC covers care only from that practice's own clinicians, so check that it fits alongside your job's plan. We walk through the HSA rules in [DPC and your HSA under the new 2026 rules](/blog/dpc-and-your-hsa-2026/).

## Can you skip the job plan and buy your own?

You can, but you may not get help paying for it. HealthCare.gov says that if your job-based offer is considered affordable and meets the minimum standard, generally a plan designed to cover at least 60% of medical costs, you may not qualify for marketplace tax credits even if your income would.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> If the offer is affordable for you but not for your family, only the other household members may qualify for savings.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> Run the numbers on HealthCare.gov before you drop a job plan.
