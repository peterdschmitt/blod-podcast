After more than 25 years, the U.S. is no longer considered measles-free. The **New York Times**' Apoorva Mandavilli and Dani Blum explained what changes in [Measles Is Officially Back in the United States. What Now?](https://gvwire.com/2026/11/17/measles-is-officially-back-in-the-united-states-what-now/) They report that "About 96% of the known cases this year have been in those who were unvaccinated or whose vaccination status was unknown." One Yale infectious disease doctor told them, "I have no confidence to think 2027 will be even better."

The question most adults are asking is simpler: am I covered? **AP**'s Laura Ungar took it on in [With measles spreading, do you need a booster shot?](https://abcnews.com/Health/wireStory/measles-spreading-booster-shot-138251807) "Until 1989, only a single dose was standard," she wrote, but Yale's Dr. Richard Martinello told her, "Most people are protected without getting a booster shot."

So here's how to figure out where you stand, and what a shot would cost you if you need one.

## What changed

The Pan American Health Organization said on Nov 17 that the U.S., Mexico and Bolivia have lost measles elimination status. The Americas have 58,213 confirmed cases this year as of Nov 13, up from 12,806 at the same point last year.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> In the U.S., CDC counts 5,412 confirmed cases as of Nov 12 and 6 deaths, a number it says may change.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> News outlets citing state data report 10 deaths.

Losing the status doesn't change how the vaccine works. It means the virus has been spreading here long enough to count as established again.

## Do you need another dose?

For most adults, no. CDC says, "One dose of MMR vaccine, or other presumptive evidence of immunity, is sufficient for most adults," and there's no CDC push for adults to get a second dose across the board.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> If you were born before 1957, CDC counts you as immune.

It's worth a conversation with a clinician if any of these fit you:

- **You got only one dose before 1989.** That's the group CNN singled out.
- **You were vaccinated between 1963 and 1967.** Some people got an inactivated vaccine then that protects less well, AP reports.
- **You can't find your records.** Getting another dose isn't generally harmful, according to doctors AP spoke with, though some people, such as pregnant women, shouldn't get it.
- **You're a college student, a health care worker or an international traveler.** CDC names these groups for vaccination if they don't have evidence of immunity.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

A blood test called a titer can show whether you have antibodies, but doctors told AP and CNN it's an imperfect measure. Don't assume your plan pays for one; ask first.

## What it costs, and who pays

If your plan follows the Affordable Care Act's preventive care rules, recommended adult immunizations, including measles, mumps and rubella, are "generally" covered "at no cost to you when provided by an in-network medical provider," and that applies "even if you haven't met your deductible." HealthCare.gov also warns that "$0 cost isn't guaranteed in all cases," so call your plan or check its preventive care list.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>

Insurers have also made a promise about the year ahead. In May, the insurer group AHIP said its "member plans will continue to cover ACIP-recommended vaccines without cost-sharing through the end of 2027," according to CIDRAP. That pledge covers recommendations made before 2025, which includes MMR.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup>

If you're uninsured or go out of network, CDC's price list puts the MMR vaccine at about $101 a dose at private-sector prices, before whatever the pharmacy or clinic charges to give the shot.<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup> Your local health department may offer lower-cost options.

## Where direct primary care fits

A direct primary care membership can make the "do I need it?" part easier: your doctor can look over your records, your birth year and your travel plans and give you a straight answer. Ask whether the practice stocks the MMR vaccine or sends you to a pharmacy. If it's a pharmacy, use your insurance there so the shot counts as covered preventive care. A DPC membership isn't insurance, and it covers only the practice's own clinicians. ## What to do now

- **Find your records.** Check a state immunization registry, an old school or college file, or a past doctor.
- **Ask a clinician if you're in one of the groups above,** especially before holiday travel abroad.
- **Get the shot in network.** That's what keeps it at $0 on most plans.
- **Don't panic if you're fully vaccinated.** Two doses are about 97% effective, CNN reports.
