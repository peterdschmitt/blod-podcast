## What counts as a value-added benefit

"Value-added benefits" is a catch-all for services packaged alongside a health plan or sold as a membership. You will see them from employers, insurers, agencies and direct-to-consumer brands. The common ones:

<div style="overflow-x: auto; margin: 0 0 24px">

| Benefit | What you actually get | Is it insurance? |
|---|---|---|
| Telehealth | 24/7 video or phone visits for minor illness, often $0 per visit | No |
| Discount prescription card | A lower cash price at the pharmacy counter | No |
| Cash-price labs and imaging | Pre-negotiated prices at labs, MRI and CT centers | No |
| Care navigation or advocacy | A person who finds providers, checks prices and fights bills | No |
| Mental health apps or counseling | A set number of sessions or app access | Usually no |
| Dental and vision discount plans | A percentage off at participating providers | No (unlike dental or vision insurance) |
| Accident, critical illness, hospital indemnity | A fixed cash payment if something specific happens | Yes, but limited ("excepted benefits") |

</div>

The last row is different. Those are insurance products, but they pay a set amount per event, not your actual bills, and they do not count as comprehensive coverage.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>

## Not insurance, on purpose

Insurance spreads the cost of rare, expensive events across many people. That machinery (claims, networks, deductibles, appeals) is expensive to run. It earns its keep on a $40,000 hospital stay. On a $35 telehealth visit or a $12 generic prescription, it mostly adds cost and delay.

Value-added benefits skip that machinery for small things. A telehealth service charges a flat fee. A discount card gives you a price. A navigator makes a phone call. That is why they can be cheap, and why they are so useful sitting next to a high-deductible plan, where small bills would otherwise come straight out of your pocket.

> The question is never "Is this insurance?" It is "Which job is this doing, and is anything else already doing it?"

## The trap: bundles sold as coverage

Some bundles stack telehealth, discount cards and a small indemnity policy, then market the package as "affordable health coverage." The National Association of Insurance Commissioners is blunt about discount plans: they "are not health insurance plans, and participants do not have the same protections as under licensed health insurance plans."<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> State regulators warn about the same thing.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>

Federal guardrails also loosened. A 2024 requirement that fixed indemnity plans carry a clear "this is not comprehensive coverage" notice was struck down in court.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> So the burden is on you to read the fine print.

## A concrete example: one bad week

Take a hypothetical 42-year-old on a bronze plan with a $7,500 deductible. She gets a sinus infection on a Saturday, then a sprained ankle the next week. Prices below are illustrative.

<div style="overflow-x: auto; margin: 0 0 24px">

| What happened | Bronze plan alone | Bronze plus value-added benefits |
|---|---|---|
| Saturday sinus infection | Urgent care, counted toward deductible: about $180 | Telehealth visit: $0 |
| Antibiotic (generic) | Pharmacy price before deductible: about $25 | Discount card price: about $8 |
| Ankle X-ray | Hospital outpatient price toward deductible: about $350 | Cash-price imaging center: about $75 |
| **Out of pocket that week** | **about $555** | **about $83** |

</div>

None of that touches the bronze plan's real job. If the ankle had been broken and needed surgery, the insurance plan pays after the deductible, up to the out-of-pocket maximum.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> The value-added benefits just keep the small stuff from eating the HSA.

One catch: cash prices paid outside your plan usually do not count toward your deductible. If you expect to hit the deductible anyway in a bad year, running everything through the plan can make more sense. In a light year, the cash route almost always wins.

## Where it overlaps with DPC

If you have direct primary care, a lot of the bundle is already covered:

<div style="overflow-x: auto; margin: 0 0 24px">

| Benefit | Usually included in DPC? | Worth buying separately? |
|---|---|---|
| Telehealth for minor illness | Yes, with your own doctor | Only for travel or nights the practice does not cover |
| Generic drugs at cost | Often, where state law allows | Discount card still useful for drugs the practice does not stock |
| Labs at wholesale price | Usually | Rarely |
| Cash-price imaging | Many practices negotiate it | Useful if yours does not |
| Care navigation | Your doctor often does it informally | Useful for hospital bills and specialists |
| Mental health counseling | Rarely | Often yes |
| Dental and vision | No | Yes, if you use them |

</div>

So the honest advice is: **list what your DPC includes first, then buy only the gaps.**

## The three-layer stack

Put together, the combination looks like this, each layer doing one job:

1. **Direct primary care** for everyday care: visits, chronic conditions, labs, generics.
2. **Value-added benefits** for the gaps DPC leaves: after-hours telehealth, imaging prices, counseling, dental and vision.
3. **An ACA insurance plan**, often bronze or catastrophic, for the big, rare bills: the ER, hospital, surgery, cancer. This is the only layer with an out-of-pocket maximum and protection for pre-existing conditions.

Telehealth before the deductible is permanently allowed alongside an HSA, so a telehealth add-on does not break HSA eligibility.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> A qualifying DPC membership no longer does either. See [DPC and your HSA](/blog/dpc-and-your-hsa-2026/).

## Who it fits, and who should skip it

- **Fits:** people on bronze, catastrophic or HSA plans who pay small bills out of pocket; families; anyone whose DPC practice has gaps listed above.
- **Skip or trim:** people on low-copay silver or gold plans, where the plan already makes small visits cheap; anyone paying for a service their DPC practice already includes.
- **Never:** as a substitute for an ACA plan if you could not pay a five-figure hospital bill.
