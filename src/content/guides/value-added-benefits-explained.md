## What counts as a value-added benefit

"Value-added benefits" is a catch-all for services packaged with a health plan or sold as a membership. Employers, insurers, agencies and direct-to-consumer brands all sell them. The common ones:

<div style="overflow-x: auto; margin: 0 0 24px">

| Benefit | What you actually get | Is it insurance? |
|---|---|---|
| Telehealth | 24/7 video or phone visits for minor illness, often $0 per visit | No |
| Discount prescription card | A lower cash price at the pharmacy counter | No |
| Cash-price labs and imaging | Pre-negotiated prices at labs, MRI and CT centers | No |
| Care navigation or advocacy | A person who finds providers, checks prices and fights bills | No |
| Mental health apps or counseling | A set number of sessions or app access | Usually no |
| Dental and vision discount plans | A percentage off at participating providers | No (unlike dental or vision insurance) |
| Accident, critical illness, hospital indemnity | A fixed cash payment if something specific happens | Yes, but limited ("excepted benefits") |

</div>

The last row is different. Those are insurance products, but they pay a set amount, such as $100 per hospital day, not your actual bills. They are not a substitute for comprehensive health insurance.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>

## Not insurance, on purpose

Insurance spreads the cost of rare, expensive events across many people. That machinery (claims, networks, deductibles, appeals) costs money to run. It earns its keep on a $40,000 hospital stay. On a $35 telehealth visit or a $12 generic prescription, it mostly adds cost and delay.

Value-added benefits skip the machinery. A telehealth service charges a flat fee. A discount card gives you a price. A navigator makes a phone call. That is why they can be cheap. It is also why they pair well with a high-deductible plan, where small bills would otherwise come straight out of your pocket.

> The question is never "Is this insurance?" It is "Which job is this doing, and is anything else already doing it?"

## The trap: bundles sold as coverage

Some sellers stack telehealth, a discount card and a small indemnity policy, then market the package as "affordable health coverage." The National Association of Insurance Commissioners is blunt about discount plans: they "are not health insurance plans, and participants do not have the same protections as under licensed health insurance plans."<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> Indiana's insurance department puts it even more simply. Discount cards "do not pay medical claims," and you pay the provider yourself at the time of care.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>

Don't count on a warning label, either. A 2024 federal rule would have required employer-offered fixed indemnity plans to carry a notice saying they are not a substitute for comprehensive coverage. A federal court in Texas struck that requirement down in December 2024.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> Reading the fine print is on you.

## A concrete example: one bad week

Picture a 42-year-old on a bronze plan with a $7,500 deductible. She gets a sinus infection on a Saturday, then sprains an ankle the next week. The prices below are illustrative, not quotes. Real prices vary a lot by town and provider.

<div style="overflow-x: auto; margin: 0 0 24px">

| What happened | Bronze plan alone | Bronze plus value-added benefits |
|---|---|---|
| Saturday sinus infection | Urgent care, counted toward deductible: about $180 | Telehealth visit: $0 |
| Antibiotic (generic) | Pharmacy price before deductible: about $25 | Discount card price: about $8 |
| Ankle X-ray | Hospital outpatient price toward deductible: about $350 | Cash-price imaging center: about $75 |
| **Out of pocket that week** | **about $555** | **about $83** (plus the bundle's monthly fee) |

</div>

None of that touches the bronze plan's real job. If the ankle had been broken and needed surgery, the plan starts paying its share after the deductible, and her costs for covered care stop at the out-of-pocket maximum.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> The add-ons just keep small bills from draining her HSA.

One catch. Cash prices paid outside your plan usually do not count toward your deductible. In a year when you expect to hit the deductible anyway, running everything through the plan can make more sense. In a light year, the cash route usually wins.

## Where it overlaps with DPC

If you have direct primary care, much of the bundle may already be covered:

<div style="overflow-x: auto; margin: 0 0 24px">

| Benefit | Usually included in DPC? | Worth buying separately? |
|---|---|---|
| Telehealth for minor illness | Yes, with your DPC practice's clinicians | Only for travel or nights the practice does not cover |
| Generic drugs at cost | Often, where state law allows | Discount card still useful for drugs the practice does not stock |
| Labs at wholesale price | Usually | Rarely |
| Cash-price imaging | Many practices negotiate it | Useful if yours does not |
| Care navigation | Your doctor often does it informally | Useful for hospital bills and specialists |
| Mental health counseling | Rarely | Often yes |
| Dental and vision | No | Yes, if you use them |

</div>

The rule of thumb: **list what your DPC practice includes first, then look only at the gaps.**

## The three-layer stack

Put together, each layer does one job:

1. **Direct primary care** for everyday care: visits, chronic conditions, labs, generics.
2. **Value-added benefits** for the gaps DPC leaves: after-hours telehealth, imaging prices, counseling, dental and vision.
3. **An ACA insurance plan** for the big, rare bills: the ER, a hospital stay, surgery, cancer. This is the only layer with an out-of-pocket maximum and protection for pre-existing conditions.

Federal law now permanently lets HSA-eligible plans cover telehealth before the deductible, so a telehealth add-on does not cost you HSA eligibility.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> Starting in 2026, a qualifying DPC membership doesn't either. See [DPC and your HSA](/blog/dpc-and-your-hsa-2026/).

## Who it tends to fit

- **Often a fit:** people on bronze, catastrophic or HSA plans who pay small bills out of pocket; families; anyone whose DPC practice has the gaps listed above.
- **Often redundant:** people on low-copay silver or gold plans, where the plan already makes small visits cheap; anyone paying for a service their DPC practice already includes.
- **Never:** a stand-in for real insurance if you could not pay a five-figure hospital bill.

Not sure which plan fits your household? Trained navigators and assisters offer free help comparing plans and enrolling. Find one through HealthCare.gov.<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup>
