## What a short-term plan is

A short-term plan is medical coverage meant to bridge a gap, like the months between jobs. It isn't an ACA plan, so the ACA's protections don't apply. It can ask about your health, turn you down, and leave out whole categories of care.

## How long you can keep one in 2027

Under the 2024 federal rule, a short-term plan sold on or after September 1, 2024 can have an initial term of no more than three months, and no more than four months in total with renewals. Plans sold before that date can keep the older limits: an initial term under 12 months and up to 36 months in total, subject to state law.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

On August 7, 2025, the federal agencies said that until new rules take effect, they don't intend to prioritize enforcing that definition, including its required notice. They said they plan to write new rules, and HHS encouraged states to take a similar approach.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> We found no new proposed or final rule published as of mid-October 2026. So the three-month limit is still on the books, but whether it's enforced, and what's sold where you live, depends heavily on your state.

## Where you can buy one

KFF found short-term plans sold in 36 states. Five states, California, Illinois, Massachusetts, New Jersey and New York, prohibit them. Nine more states and Washington, D.C. allow them, but none are available because of other state rules.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>

## What they leave out

KFF reviewed 30 short-term products from nine insurers.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> Among them:

The money limits matter just as much. Deductibles ran from $500 to $25,000. Plans with no out-of-pocket maximum were available in every city KFF checked but one, and some plans capped total benefits at $100,000 per policy term.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>

## What it costs

The low price is real. KFF found the cheapest short-term plans often cost two-thirds or less of the cheapest unsubsidized bronze plan in the same area. But most marketplace enrollees get subsidies, which can make a bronze plan the cheaper choice.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> If you qualify for any tax credit, check HealthCare.gov before you compare.

Here's how a big bill can play out. Take Riley, a hypothetical 35-year-old with a short-term plan that has a $10,000 deductible, 20% coinsurance and no out-of-pocket maximum. Riley has a hospital stay billed at $40,000 after the plan's discounts, for a condition the plan covers.

- Riley pays the deductible: $10,000
- Then 20% of the remaining $30,000: $6,000
- Riley's total: $16,000

An ACA-compliant plan for 2027 can't make one person pay more than $12,000 in a year for covered in-network care. And if the insurer decides the condition started before the policy did, the short-term plan may pay nothing at all.

## Questions to ask before you buy

- **How long can I keep it in my state, and can I renew?**
- **Does it exclude preexisting conditions, and how far back does it look?**
- **What's the deductible, and is there an out-of-pocket maximum?**
- **Does it cover prescriptions, mental health and maternity care?**
- **Is there a cap on what it pays in total?**

Some people pair a short-term plan with a direct primary care membership for everyday visits. DPC covers care only from that practice's own clinicians, and it doesn't fix a short-term plan's gaps for hospital care, prescriptions or preexisting conditions.
