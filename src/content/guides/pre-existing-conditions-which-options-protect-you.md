People with health conditions have felt this year's price jumps the hardest. **ABC News** reported in March that [1 in 10 ACA enrollees dropped their coverage due to rising health care costs](https://abcnews.com/Health/1-10-aca-enrollees-dropped-coverage-due-rising/story?id=131178596), and Mary Kekatos quotes one woman who went uninsured: "This is destroying people who have pre-existing conditions."

Some people are looking at cheaper plans instead. **CBS News** reported in May that [Cheaper, alternative health plans are having a moment, but critics urge caution](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/aca-health-insurance-alternative-plans-cheaper/), noting that "state oversight of alternative insurance is a patchwork."

That's the problem in a sentence. If you have a condition, a lower premium can come with a catch that's easy to miss: the plan may not cover the very thing you need care for. A KFF estimate based on 2018 data found about 27% of adults under 65, about 53.8 million people, have a health condition that insurers would have declined before the ACA.<sup><a href="#src-12">12</a></sup> Here's how each option treats your history.

## Options that must cover your conditions

**Marketplace plans.** "All Marketplace plans must cover treatment for pre-existing medical conditions," HealthCare.gov says. "Once you're enrolled, the plan can't deny you coverage or raise your rates based only on your health."<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> Catastrophic plans are marketplace plans too, so the same rule applies. The exception is a grandfathered plan bought on or before March 23, 2010, which doesn't have to cover pre-existing conditions.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

**Job-based plans, including COBRA.** "Group health plans are prohibited from imposing any preexisting condition exclusion," the Labor Department says.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

**Medicaid and CHIP.** They also can't refuse to cover you or charge you more because of a pre-existing condition.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

## Options that may not

**Short-term plans.** CMS says they aren't subject to the ACA's bans on health-status discrimination and pre-existing condition exclusions.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> State regulators add that "STLDI policies are often medically underwritten," meaning the insurer looks at your health before it says yes.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> Federal limits on how long these plans can last aren't being enforced right now, so your state's rules matter most.

**Health care sharing ministries.** They aren't insurance, and "the ministries are not legally or contractually obligated to pay health care costs," Michigan's regulator says.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> Nebraska warns members they may face pre-existing condition restrictions.<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup>

**Fixed indemnity and critical illness policies.** These pay set cash amounts, not your bills. Maryland's regulator says an indemnity policy "may not include coverage for your pre-existing conditions."<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup> One critical illness policy we read counts any condition you got care or advice for in the 12 months before coverage starts, and pays nothing for it in the first 12 months.<sup><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup> Read the exclusions page before you buy.

## Where DPC fits

A direct primary care membership isn't insurance, so it won't pay for a hospital stay or a specialist. But some states protect patients from being screened out. Washington's law says "direct practices may not decline to accept new direct patients or discontinue care to existing patients solely because of the patient's health status." A practice can still say no if it's full or if your condition needs care it can't provide.<sup><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup> Rules differ by state, so ask any practice directly. Keep in mind a membership covers only that practice's own clinicians, so unless your current doctor is part of it, joining means switching.

For people with a condition, DPC tends to work best alongside a plan that must cover you, like a marketplace bronze plan, rather than in place of one.

## At 65: the Medigap window

When you turn 65 and have Part B, you get a 6-month Medigap Open Enrollment Period. During it, insurers can't use medical underwriting to decide whether to accept you.<sup><a href="#src-10">10</a></sup> After it ends, "the insurance company is allowed to deny you a policy if you don't meet their medical underwriting requirements," Medicare.gov says.<sup><a href="#src-10">10</a></sup> KFF found that 74% of Medicare Advantage enrollees, 20.9 million people in 2023, could be denied a Medigap policy over a pre-existing condition if they tried to switch later. Only four states, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine and New York, require insurers to offer Medigap to everyone 65 and older year-round or once a year.<sup><a href="#src-11">11</a></sup>

## Questions to ask before you switch

- **Does this plan ask health questions or check my medical records?** If yes, it can turn you down.
- **Is there a lookback period or a waiting period for pre-existing conditions?** Get the months in writing.
- **Is it insurance, and who regulates it?** Your state insurance department can tell you.
- **If my marketplace coverage is under review, what's my deadline to respond?** Answer any notice from HealthCare.gov or your state marketplace right away.
