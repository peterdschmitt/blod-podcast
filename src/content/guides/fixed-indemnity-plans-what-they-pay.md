## What a fixed indemnity plan is

A fixed indemnity plan pays you a set amount of money when something specific happens, like a night in the hospital or a doctor visit. The amount is fixed in advance. Federal rules say it's paid "regardless of the amount of expenses incurred," and give $100 a day or $50 a visit as examples.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup><sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> The plan pays the same whether your bill is $500 or $50,000.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>

That makes it an "excepted benefit," a category that sits outside the Affordable Care Act's rules for health insurance. Here's what that means for you:

- **Preexisting conditions.** These plans can, and do, turn down people with preexisting health conditions. Even if you're accepted, care tied to a preexisting condition is typically excluded.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>
- **Caps.** Benefits are almost always limited, with per-incident, annual or lifetime caps.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>
- **No out-of-pocket limit.** The plan doesn't owe your bill, so nothing caps what you owe the hospital. You get the fixed payment, and the rest is yours.

## The math on a hospital stay

KFF puts the average expense for US hospitals at $3,297 per inpatient day in 2024. It ranges from about $1,401 in Mississippi to $4,744 in California.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> That's what it costs the hospital, so your bill can be higher.

Take Dana, a hypothetical 45-year-old with a fixed indemnity plan that pays $100 a day in the hospital, and no other coverage. She spends three days in the hospital.

- Hospital expense at the national average: $3,297 × 3 = $9,891
- Her plan pays: $100 × 3 = $300
- Left for Dana, before any doctor's bills: $9,891 − $300 = $9,591

The 2024 federal rule describes a real plan that paid $300 for neonatal intensive care, when one hospital charged $8,500 a day for it.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

## The warning label, and why you may not see it

In 2024, federal agencies required a bold notice on these plans: "IMPORTANT: This is a fixed indemnity policy, NOT health insurance."<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup> A federal court in Texas struck down that requirement on December 4, 2024.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> The agencies appealed, then dropped the appeal, and it was dismissed on June 10, 2025, so the new notice is no longer in force.<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup>

What's left:

- **Individual policies** still carry the older notice: "THIS IS A SUPPLEMENT TO HEALTH INSURANCE AND IS NOT A SUBSTITUTE FOR MAJOR MEDICAL COVERAGE."<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup><sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup>
- **Group plans** sold through an employer carry no federal notice at all.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup>

So don't count on a warning label. Read what the plan pays, not what it's called.

## Why you may hear about one this fall

KFF has warned that consumers could be steered by marketers and insurers to fixed indemnity plans, along with cancer-only and hospital-only plans.<sup><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup> Secret-shopper studies have routinely found sellers of these plans using aggressive tactics to close a quick sale. Some sellers of these and similar plans tell buyers that enrollment will fill up or the price will rise if they wait.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> If someone tells you to decide today, that's a reason to slow down.

## Where it can fit

A fixed indemnity plan isn't useless. As cash on top of real coverage, a daily payment can help with a deductible, travel or lost wages during a hospital stay. The older notice says it plainly: it's a supplement, not a substitute.<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup>

The same goes for a direct primary care membership. DPC covers everyday visits with that practice's own clinicians for a flat fee, and neither DPC nor a fixed indemnity plan pays a big hospital bill. If you're building a lower-cost setup, the piece that handles the big bills is the insurance plan. Marketplace open enrollment runs November 1, 2026 to January 15, 2027.

Questions to ask before you buy:

- **What does it pay, in dollars, for a day in the hospital, a surgery and an ER visit?**
- **What are the per-incident, yearly and lifetime caps?**
- **Does it exclude preexisting conditions, and for how long?**
- **Is this my only coverage?** If so, compare it with a marketplace plan first.
