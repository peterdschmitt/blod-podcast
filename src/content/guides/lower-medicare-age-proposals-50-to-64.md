A bigger Medicare is suddenly part of the conversation again. **AP**'s Joey Cappelletti and Fatima Hussein reported on November 18 that [Bernie Sanders unveils his plan to push Medicare for All when Democrats take power](https://www.inquirer.com/politics/nation/bernie-sanders-medicare-for-all-democrats-20261118.html). "The first year would lower the Medicare eligibility age from 65 to 55," they wrote, with coverage reaching everyone in the fourth year.

The coverage you have now is in the news, too. **CBS News** ran Julie Appleby's KFF Health News report, [Big win sets up Democrats to intensify probe of Trump's Obamacare purge](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/democrats-probe-trump-obamacare-coverage-cancellations/), on Senate Democrats questioning insurers about the cancellation of coverage for "760,000 Affordable Care Act enrollees in late August."

If you're between 50 and 64, here's what's actually on the table, and what it means for the choice in front of you this month.

## The three ideas, in plain terms

- **Lower the age.** Jeffries says, "We should lower the age of Medicare eligibility," and wants to add vision, hearing and dental coverage. He hasn't said to what age.
- **Phase in Medicare for everyone.** Sanders' plan starts at 55 in year one, then 45, then 35, then everyone.
- **Let people buy in early.** The Medicare at 50 Act from Sens. Jack Reed and Tammy Baldwin would let people ages 50 to 64 buy into Medicare Parts A, B and D, or choose a Medicare Advantage plan. Buyers could get "the same premium tax credits and cost-sharing subsidies they would qualify for through the ACA Marketplace."<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> The release doesn't say what the premium would be or when it would start.

## What a change could mean, and what it couldn't

A buy-in isn't free Medicare. You'd still pay a premium, the way you would for a marketplace plan.

The only official cost estimate is old and covers a different design. In 2022, CBO estimated that lowering the age to 60 would add $155 billion to federal deficits over 2026 to 2031. It also found that most people who would sign up already have insurance and would mainly switch where it comes from.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> That matches older KFF data on people ages 60 to 64. About two-thirds had private coverage, and most of the uninsured in that group were already eligible for financial help.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>

## What to do now

- **Don't wait on Congress for 2027.** No proposal changes your options for next year.
- **Shop the marketplace if you buy your own coverage.** Pick by Dec 15 for a Jan 1 start. Our guide to [2027 open enrollment dates and changes](/blog/open-enrollment-2027-dates-and-changes/) walks through the calendar.
- **Check your subsidy.** If your premium help shrank or disappeared, see [Lost your subsidy? 2027 options, side by side](/blog/lost-your-subsidy-2027-options/).
- **Keep everyday care steady.** A direct primary care membership works alongside whatever plan you choose. It isn't insurance, and it covers only the practice's own clinicians.
