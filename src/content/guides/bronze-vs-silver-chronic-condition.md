Fewer people are holding on to coverage they buy themselves. **Reuters** reported on October 14 that [US individual health insurance enrollment fell to 20.5 million as of June, KFF says](https://1027wbow.com/2026/10/14/obamacare-enrollment-fell-to-20-5-million-people-as-of-june-kff-says/). Amina Niasse writes that enrollment fell 18% from 25.2 million a year earlier, after the extra pandemic-era subsidies expired.

Plan shopping is getting harder on the Medicare side too. **CNN**'s Tami Luhby reported on October 15 that [More than 3 million Medicare Advantage enrollees need to find new plans for 2027 in third year of turmoil](https://krdo.com/politics/cnn-us-politics/2026/10/15/more-than-3-million-medicare-advantage-enrollees-need-to-find-new-plans-for-2027-in-third-year-of-turmoil). Some 70% of enrollees will see their out-of-pocket cap go up next year.

If you buy your own coverage, open enrollment starts November 1. This year the share of marketplace shoppers picking bronze jumped from 30% to 40%, while silver fell from 57% to 43%.<sup><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup> If you have a chronic condition, that move deserves a second look.

## The basic difference

Every marketplace plan is sorted into a metal tier by how much of your covered care it pays for on average. A bronze plan pays about 60% and leaves you about 40%. A silver plan pays about 70% and leaves you about 30%.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> Bronze trades a lower premium for a higher deductible. In 2026 the average deductible was $7,186 for a bronze plan and $5,304 for silver.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>

Those averages hide what matters most when you need care all year: which services the plan pays for before you meet the deductible.

## What gets covered before the deductible

For 2027, CMS set out standardized plan designs that insurers on HealthCare.gov must offer alongside their other plans. Here's how the standard bronze and silver versions compare.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>

<div style="overflow-x: auto; margin: 0 0 24px">

| | Expanded Bronze | Standard Silver |
|---|---|---|
| Deductible | $7,500 | $6,400 |
| Out-of-pocket maximum | $12,000 | $10,300 |
| Primary care visit | $50, before the deductible | $40, before the deductible |
| Specialist visit | $100, before the deductible | $80, before the deductible |
| Generic drugs | $25, before the deductible | $20, before the deductible |
| Preferred brand-name drugs | $50, after the deductible | $40, before the deductible |
| Lab work | 50%, after the deductible | 40%, after the deductible |

</div>

The row that matters most for a lot of chronic conditions is brand-name drugs. On the standard bronze plan, you pay the full negotiated price for a preferred brand-name drug until you've spent $7,500. On the standard silver plan, you pay $40 a fill from day one.

These are the standardized designs. Insurers also sell their own plans, and those can cover more or less before the deductible, so check each plan's summary of benefits.

## If your income is under 250% of the poverty line

Silver has an advantage bronze can't match. If your income is between 100% and 250% of the poverty line and you qualify for a premium tax credit, you can get cost-sharing reductions, and they come only with silver plans.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> They shrink the deductible and the out-of-pocket maximum. In the 2027 standardized designs:<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>

- **Silver 94** (the lowest incomes): $0 deductible, $3,500 out-of-pocket maximum
- **Silver 87**: $800 deductible, $3,600 out-of-pocket maximum
- **Silver 73**: $2,900 deductible, $9,000 out-of-pocket maximum

If you have a chronic condition and your income is in this range, a silver plan is usually worth pricing first. Until November 1, HealthCare.gov suggests using 2026 income amounts to get a rough idea of what you'd save in 2027.

## A worked year, two ways

Take Jordan, a hypothetical 40-year-old with no subsidy and a condition that needs regular care. We use our [calculator's](/calculator/) assumed 2027 premiums for a 40-year-old: $525 a month for bronze and $720 a month for silver. That's $2,340 a year more for silver. These are assumptions, not quotes.

**Year A: a brand-name drug.** Jordan sees a primary care doctor 4 times, a specialist twice, fills a generic every month, fills a preferred brand-name drug priced at $500 a month, and gets 2 lab panels at $150 each.

<div style="overflow-x: auto; margin: 0 0 24px">

| | Expanded Bronze | Standard Silver |
|---|---|---|
| 4 primary care visits | 4 × $50 = $200 | 4 × $40 = $160 |
| 2 specialist visits | 2 × $100 = $200 | 2 × $80 = $160 |
| 12 generic fills | 12 × $25 = $300 | 12 × $20 = $240 |
| 12 brand-name fills | $6,000 at full price, under the deductible | 12 × $40 = $480 |
| 2 lab panels | $300, under the deductible | $300, under the deductible |
| **Out of pocket** | **$7,000** | **$1,340** |
| Premiums for the year | $6,300 | $8,640 |
| **Total** | **$13,300** | **$9,980** |

</div>

Silver comes out $3,320 ahead. The bronze plan's lower premium is wiped out by the brand-name drug sitting under the deductible.

**Year B: generics only.** Same Jordan, but the condition is managed with 2 primary care visits, a monthly generic and 1 lab panel.

- Bronze out of pocket: $100 + $300 + $150 = $550, plus $6,300 in premiums, for $6,850
- Silver out of pocket: $80 + $240 + $150 = $470, plus $8,640 in premiums, for $9,110

Bronze comes out $2,260 ahead. When most of your care already gets a copay before the deductible, the lower premium wins.

The lesson isn't that one tier is better. It's that the answer depends on what your care actually is, and especially on your drugs.

## Where an HSA and DPC fit

Since January 1, 2026, bronze and catastrophic plans bought through the marketplace count as HSA-compatible.<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup> Money you put in an HSA goes in before tax, which lowers the real cost of everything you pay under the deductible. That can narrow the gap in Year A, but it doesn't close it.

A direct primary care membership can take over the primary care visits and often offers labs and generics at low cash prices. It won't pay for a brand-name drug, a specialist or a hospital stay, so it doesn't fix Jordan's Year A problem. A membership also covers only that practice's own clinicians, so unless your current doctor is part of it, joining means switching.<sup><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup> For the full DPC-plus-bronze math, see [DPC plus a bronze plan, with worked numbers](/blog/dpc-plus-bronze-worked-numbers/).

## Five things to check before you pick

- **Is each of your drugs on the plan's formulary, and in which tier?** The plan's website should link to its list of covered drugs.
- **Do your drugs get a copay before the deductible, or do you pay full price first?**
- **Are your doctors and specialists in the plan's network?**
- **What's the out-of-pocket maximum?** For 2027, no marketplace plan can make one person pay more than $12,000 for covered in-network care.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup>
- **Does your income qualify you for cost-sharing reductions on silver?**

Then add up a realistic year, premiums plus what you'd pay for the care you know you'll need. Many people with chronic conditions are already stretched. In a KFF survey this spring, 62% of marketplace enrollees with a chronic condition said they are cutting back, or will cut back, on food and other basics.<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup> Picking the plan that's cheaper over the whole year is one place to win some of that back.
