It isn't only marketplace shoppers feeling it. **NPR**'s Selena Simmons-Duffin reported on October 22 that [High health costs squeeze workers, even if you have good insurance through a job](https://www.opb.org/article/2026/10/22/even-workers-with-good-health-insurance-struggle-with-high-costs/). One finance manager told her his company's deductibles were $250 or $500 in the 1990s and are $5,000 now.

When deductibles are that big, the plan with the lowest monthly price can be the one that costs you most. Here's a way to compare two plans on what you'd actually pay in a year.

## The four numbers on every plan

Every marketplace plan shows the same handful of costs. The premium is what you pay each month, even in a month you don't see a doctor. The deductible is what you pay for certain covered care each year before the plan starts paying its share. After that, copays and coinsurance are your part of each bill.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

The fourth number is the out-of-pocket maximum. It's the most you pay for covered, in-network care in a plan year. Once you hit it, the plan pays 100% of covered benefits. For 2027, no ACA plan can set it higher than $12,000 for one person or $24,000 for a family. Premiums don't count toward it, and neither does out-of-network care or anything the plan doesn't cover.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

HealthCare.gov's own advice is to think about both kinds of costs, the premium and what you pay when you get care.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> That matters more each year. KFF data shows the average marketplace deductible rose from $2,759 in 2025 to $3,786 in 2026, and more people picked bronze plans, which usually have the lowest premiums and the highest costs when you get care.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup><sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup>

## Three years to price out

You can't know what next year holds, so price out three versions of it for each plan.

- **A quiet year.** A checkup, a couple of sick visits, a generic prescription. Add 12 months of premium to the little you'd pay out of pocket.
- **A busy year.** Something unplanned but not catastrophic, like an MRI and physical therapy, or a short procedure. Use a round number you could see happening, say $5,000 to $8,000 of care.
- **A bad year.** A hospital stay or a new diagnosis. Here the math is easy: 12 months of premium plus the out-of-pocket maximum.

The bad-year number is the one most people skip, and it's the easiest to compare. It's the most the plan could cost you for covered in-network care.

## A worked example, two plans

Take Morgan, a hypothetical 45-year-old buying on her own, comparing two made-up plans. The premiums are what she'd pay after any tax credit.

<div style="overflow-x: auto">

| | Plan A | Plan B |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly premium | $520 | $640 |
| Premiums for the year | $6,240 | $7,680 |
| Deductible | $7,500 | $3,000 |
| Coinsurance after deductible | 40% | 20% |
| Out-of-pocket maximum | $10,000 | $8,000 |

</div>

Plan A looks $120 a month cheaper. Here's how the three years come out.

- **Quiet year, $600 of care.** Morgan pays the $600 under either plan. Plan A: $6,240 + $600 = $6,840. Plan B: $7,680 + $600 = $8,280. Plan A saves $1,440.
- **Busy year, $6,000 of care.** Under Plan A she's still inside the deductible and pays all $6,000, for $12,240 total. Under Plan B she pays the $3,000 deductible plus 20% of the next $3,000 ($600), for $3,600 out of pocket and $11,280 total. Plan B saves $960.
- **Bad year, a big hospital bill.** Plan A: $6,240 + $10,000 = $16,240. Plan B: $7,680 + $8,000 = $15,680. Plan B saves $560.

So where's the tipping point? Plan B costs $1,440 more in premiums, and it starts paying sooner. Once Morgan's care tops about $4,800 in a year, Plan B's lower deductible makes up the difference. Below that, Plan A wins. Your plans will have different numbers, but the method is the same. Our [cost calculator](/calculator/) runs this kind of comparison with a DPC membership added in.

## If your income is under 250% of the poverty line

Look hard at silver. If your household income is up to 250% of the federal poverty line, a silver plan comes with cost-sharing reductions, a discount that lowers your deductible, copays, coinsurance and out-of-pocket maximum. You only get them if you pick a silver plan.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup><sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> In the three-year test, that can turn silver from the middle choice into the cheapest one in both the busy year and the bad year.

## Where DPC fits in the math

A direct primary care membership is a monthly fee for unlimited visits with that practice's own clinicians. It isn't insurance, and it covers care only from that practice, so joining usually means switching doctors unless yours is already part of it. In the three-year test it shows up as a fixed cost, like a second premium. What it can change is the quiet year, because routine visits no longer count against your deductible. We walk through a full year of that pairing in [DPC plus a bronze plan, with worked numbers](/blog/dpc-plus-bronze-worked-numbers/).

## Five things to check before you pick

- **Is your doctor in the network?** Out-of-network care doesn't count toward the in-network out-of-pocket limit.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>
- **Are your prescriptions covered, and at what tier?**
- **What do you pay before the deductible?** Some plans cover visits or generics with a copay from day one.
- **Did your plan change for 2027?** If you let it renew without looking, you may miss a better fit.
- **What's the deadline?** Open enrollment runs November 1, 2026 to January 15, 2027 on HealthCare.gov. Pick by December 15 for coverage that starts January 1.<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup>
