Health care costs are now the issue voters worry about most this fall. **Bloomberg** put it in one line on October 6: [Healthcare Costs Beat Housing as Top Voter Midterm Concern](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-10-06/healthcare-costs-beat-housing-as-top-voter-midterm-concern). Three days earlier, the **Associated Press** reported that [Trump announces payments to seniors to help pay Medicare premiums ahead of midterm elections](https://www.wowt.com/2026/10/03/trump-announces-payments-seniors-help-pay-medicare-premiums-ahead-midterm-elections/). The one-time $90 payment goes to more than 20 million people in Medicare Part B.

Checks and campaign promises come and go. The thing that actually sets what you pay next year is a calendar. If you buy your own coverage, the window to choose a 2027 plan opens November 1, and a few dates inside it decide when your coverage starts and what it costs.

Here are those dates, what changes for 2027, and what happens if you let the window pass.

## The dates that matter

On HealthCare.gov, which serves most states, open enrollment runs from November 1, 2026 to January 15, 2027.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup><sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> Within that window, the date you pick a plan decides when it starts.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

<div style="overflow-x: auto; margin: 0 0 24px">

| When you enroll or change plans | When the 2027 plan starts |
|---|---|
| November 1 to December 15, 2026 | January 1, 2027 |
| December 16, 2026 to January 15, 2027 | February 1, 2027, once you pay the first premium |
| After January 15, 2027 | Only with a special enrollment period, such as after losing other coverage |

</div>

States that run their own exchange can set their own dates. Idaho opens October 15 and closes December 15. New York, Illinois, Pennsylvania and the District of Columbia stay open to January 31. Massachusetts and Connecticut open October 23.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> Oregon is moving off HealthCare.gov to its own platform for 2027.<sup><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup> If you're not sure which system your state uses, check before November 1.

Medicare is separate. If someone in your household has Medicare, their open enrollment runs October 15 to December 7, and changes take effect January 1.<sup><a href="#src-10">10</a></sup>

## What changes for 2027

**Prices.** Insurers proposed a median premium increase of 15% for 2027.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> If you get a premium tax credit, part of that may be absorbed, because the credit is tied to the price of a benchmark plan. If you pay full price, you feel all of it.<sup><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup>

**Insurers.** KFF counts 11 insurers that have announced exits from some or all of their marketplaces for 2027. Cigna is leaving all 11 states where it sells individual plans.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> If your insurer is leaving your state, your current plan won't be offered in 2027.

**Caps and accounts.** No marketplace plan can make one person pay more than $12,000 out of pocket for covered care in 2027, or $24,000 for a family.<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup> Bronze and catastrophic plans still work with a health savings account, and the 2027 contribution limits are $4,500 for one person and $9,000 for a family.<sup><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup><sup><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup>

**What didn't change.** A federal court in Maryland paused several 2027 rule changes in July, including wider eligibility for catastrophic plans and higher out-of-pocket limits for bronze plans. CMS has said open enrollment still runs November 1 to January 15.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> If you're 30 or older, you still need a hardship or affordability exemption to buy a catastrophic plan.<sup><a href="#src-9">9</a></sup>

## If you do nothing

HealthCare.gov will automatically re-enroll you so you don't have a gap in coverage. You might stay in your plan or land in a different one. If your insurer has no similar plan for 2027, you'll be moved to a comparable plan from another insurer.<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup> Either way, you pay the 2027 price.

HealthCare.gov also says to update your application with your expected 2027 income and household, so you get the savings you qualify for.<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup> Automatic renewal won't do that thinking for you.

## One household, two choices

Take Luis, a hypothetical 52-year-old who pays the full price of $600 a month for a 2026 plan. His insurer is one of the ones leaving his state.

If he waits, he's moved to a comparable plan. Say its 2027 price is 15% higher than what he pays now:

- $600 × 1.15 = $690 a month
- $90 more each month, or $1,080 more for the year

If he shops in November instead, he sees every 2027 plan in his area side by side, including plans with lower premiums and higher deductibles. He has to choose by December 15 to have his new plan in place on January 1. If he decides on January 10 instead, the switch takes effect February 1.

Neither choice is automatically right. A cheaper plan with a bigger deductible can cost more in a year with a lot of care. The point is that Luis gets to compare only if he acts inside the window. For ways to weigh premium against deductible, see [DPC plus a bronze plan, with worked numbers](/blog/dpc-plus-bronze-worked-numbers/). You can also run your own numbers in the [stack calculator](/calculator/).

## Your checklist before November 1

- **Find your exchange and its dates.** HealthCare.gov, or your state's site if it runs one.
- **Check the letter from your insurer.** It tells you if your plan is ending or changing.
- **Estimate your 2027 income.** Subsidies are based on next year's income, not this year's.
- **List your doctors and prescriptions.** Check them against each plan's network and drug list before you pick one.
- **Put December 15 on your calendar.** That's the date for coverage on January 1.
