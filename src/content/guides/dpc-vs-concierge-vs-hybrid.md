Some patients are trying something else: paying a practice directly for more of the doctor's time. If you're shopping for one, you'll see three labels: direct primary care, concierge medicine and "hybrid." They sound alike, and they all charge a membership fee. The differences show up in your insurance, your Medicare and your HSA.

## The quick difference

<div style="overflow-x: auto; margin: 0 0 24px">

| | Direct primary care | Concierge | Hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Membership fee | Monthly, often $50 to $100 | Yearly retainer, often thousands | Varies |
| Bills your insurance for the care the fee covers | No | Can, for covered services | Often, yes |
| Medicare pays the fee | No | No | No |
| Counts as DPC for your HSA | Can, if it meets IRS rules | No, if it bills insurance for that care | No, if it bills insurance for that care |

</div>

The table pulls from AAFP, Medicare.gov and IRS guidance, cited in each section below.

## Direct primary care

A DPC practice charges you a flat periodic fee and doesn't bill insurance for the care that fee covers. AAFP describes DPC as an alternative to fee-for-service insurance billing, with monthly fees that generally range from $50 to $100.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> Visits, messages and calls with the practice's own clinicians come with the membership.

Because nothing goes through insurance for that care, there's no per-message claim and no copay at the door. The trade-off is that DPC isn't insurance. AAFP notes practices often suggest a high-deductible policy to cover emergencies.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> A membership also covers only that practice's clinicians, so unless your current doctor is part of it, joining means switching doctors.<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup>

## Concierge medicine

A concierge practice charges a membership fee before it will see you,<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> and AAFP says it can still bill your insurance company for covered services.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> So you may pay twice: the retainer for access, and your usual copays and deductible for the visits.

Retainers run from a few thousand dollars to tens of thousands of dollars a year, according to a Johns Hopkins study published in Health Affairs.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> The same study found concierge and DPC practice sites grew 83.1% from 2018 to 2023, from 1,658 to 3,036.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

## Hybrid practices

"Hybrid" isn't a legal category. It usually means a practice that bills insurance like a traditional office and also sells a membership for extras, or keeps some patients on insurance and others on memberships. The label tells you little, so ask one direct question: **does the practice bill my insurance for the same care my fee pays for?** If it does, it works like concierge care for your wallet.

## If you're on Medicare

Medicare doesn't cover membership fees for concierge care, and you pay all costs for non-covered services, including that fee.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> Federal officials have also drawn a line on what the fee can buy. In 2004, the HHS Office of Inspector General warned doctors that asking Medicare patients for extra payment for services Medicare already covers can bring civil money penalties and exclusion from Medicare. Fees for services Medicare doesn't cover are allowed.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> If a practice that bills Medicare asks you for a membership fee, ask for a written list of what it buys.

## If you have an HSA

Starting in 2026, an HSA can pay a DPC fee, and having DPC no longer blocks you from contributing. The arrangement has to meet IRS rules: it covers only primary care from primary care practitioners, and a fixed periodic fee is the sole payment for that care.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> A practice that bills separately for those same services, "through insurance or otherwise," doesn't qualify.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> The fee can't top $150 a month for one person or $300 for more than one, and those caps hold for 2027.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup><sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup>

A DPC practice can still bill insurance for care outside the membership and qualify.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> What matters is the care your fee covers. Concierge and hybrid fees for care that's also billed to insurance usually won't count. For the full rules, see [DPC and your HSA in 2026](/blog/dpc-and-your-hsa-2026/).

## What a year could cost

Take Sam, a hypothetical 40-year-old who sees a primary care doctor four times a year and sends a handful of portal messages. These are illustrations, not quotes.

- **DPC at $75 a month:** $75 × 12 = $900 a year, with visits and messages included. An HSA can pay it if the practice meets the IRS rules.
- **Concierge at a $2,000 yearly retainer:** $2,000, plus whatever Sam's insurance plan charges for each visit.
- **Traditional office:** no fee, but each visit costs whatever Sam's insurance plan charges, and some practices bill insurance for portal messages too.

Which one is worth it depends on how often you use primary care and what your insurance already covers.

## Questions that tell you which one you're looking at

- **Do you bill my insurance for anything my membership covers?**
- **What exactly does the fee include, and what costs extra?**
- **Are you enrolled in Medicare, or have you opted out?**
- **Does your fee meet the IRS rules for HSA payment?**
- **How many patients does each doctor see?**
