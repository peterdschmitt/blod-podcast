The people getting the government's new $500 health insurance checks share one thing: they earn too much for help. **NPR** told one of their stories on October 7, [She got Trump's $500 check for health insurance. To her, it's a 'political stunt'](https://www.kpbs.org/news/health/2026/10/07/she-got-trumps-500-check-for-health-insurance-to-her-its-a-political-stunt). The checks went to people making more than $64,000 a year as individuals, too much to qualify for subsidies. One of them, Ellen Allen, paid $1,967 a month for her premiums from January through August, out of retirement savings.

The same day, **KFF Health News** reported from Michigan that ['Medicare for All' Message Attracts Voters Buckling Under Medical Bills](https://kffhealthnews.org/elections/medicare-for-all-abdul-el-sayed-michigan-midterm-elections/). Insurers there raised individual marketplace rates by an average of 20%.

Both stories turn on the same line. Earn under it and a tax credit caps what you pay. Earn a dollar over it and you pay full price. With 2027 shopping starting November 1, here is exactly where that line sits and what crossing it costs.

## Where the line sits for 2027

Tax credits for 2027 coverage use the poverty guidelines HHS published in January 2026.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> The credit stops at 400% of those numbers:

<div style="overflow-x: auto; margin: 0 0 24px">

| Household size | 400% of the poverty line |
|---|---|
| 1 | $63,840 |
| 2 | $86,560 |
| 4 | $132,000 |

</div>

Alaska and Hawaii have higher figures.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> The income that counts is your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) for 2027, which you estimate when you apply.

## How the credit works right under the line

Between 300% and 400% of the poverty line, the IRS sets your expected share at 10.22% of income for 2027.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup> The credit covers the rest of the price of your area's benchmark plan, the second-cheapest silver plan. You can put the credit toward a different plan, but it's sized to that benchmark.

So at exactly $63,840, one person pays no more than about $6,524 a year for the benchmark plan. At $63,841, the credit is zero. Nothing phases it out gradually.

## Three hypothetical households

To show the cliff, we estimated 2027 benchmark prices. KFF puts the average 2026 benchmark premium for a 40-year-old at $625 a month.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> We adjusted it by age using the federal default age curve, which applies in states that don't set their own,<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> then added the 15% median increase insurers proposed for 2027.<sup><a href="#src-6">6</a></sup> These are national averages, not quotes. Your ZIP code can move them a lot.

<div style="overflow-x: auto; margin: 0 0 24px">

| Household | Estimated 2027 benchmark, full price | You pay just under the line | Cost of crossing the line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jordan, 30, single | $638 a month, $7,656 a year | $6,439 a year (at $63,000) | about $1,217 a year |
| Linda, 60, single | $1,526 a month, $18,312 a year | $6,439 a year (at $63,000) | about $11,873 a year |
| Pat and Sam, both 62 | $3,232 a month, $38,784 a year | $8,789 a year (at $86,000) | about $29,995 a year |

</div>

The math, step by step:

- **Jordan:** 10.22% of $63,000 is $6,439. The credit is $7,656 minus $6,439, or $1,217. If a raise pushes Jordan to $64,500, the whole credit disappears. That stings, but a young person's full price isn't far above the cap anyway.
- **Linda:** her share is the same $6,439. Her full price is almost three times Jordan's, so her credit is $11,873. If her income rises $1,000, to $64,000, she pays $11,873 more for the same plan.
- **Pat and Sam:** 10.22% of $86,000 is $8,789. Their credit is $38,784 minus $8,789, or $29,995. Their line is $86,560, so a $2,000 bonus would put them over it and cost them nearly $30,000 in credits.

Same rule, very different stakes. KFF found the same pattern for 2026: it estimated that a 60-year-old earning $62,000 would pay about $6,175 a year, while one earning $64,000 would pay $14,931.<sup><a href="#src-7">7</a></sup>

## Guessing wrong now costs more

You get the credit in advance, based on your income estimate. At tax time, the IRS settles up. Starting with tax years after 2025, there's no longer a cap on how much of an overpaid credit you pay back.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> If Linda estimates $63,000, gets the credit all year, then ends up at $64,000, she could owe back the full $11,873.

If your income swings, update your marketplace application during the year when it changes, rather than waiting for tax season.

## Ways to land under the line

Money that comes off your MAGI can pull you back under the cutoff:

- **HSA contributions:** up to $4,500 for one person or $9,000 for a family in 2027, plus $1,000 if you're 55 or older.<sup><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup> You need an HSA-compatible plan, and every marketplace bronze plan now counts.
- **Traditional (pre-tax) 401(k), IRA, SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k) contributions.**

Pat and Sam, at $88,000, are $1,440 over. Putting $2,000 into an HSA, well within the $9,000 family limit, would bring them back under. We walk through these moves in [Lost your subsidy? 2027 options, side by side](/blog/lost-your-subsidy-2027-options/). Check with a tax professional before you count on them.

## Where DPC fits

If you land on a bronze plan with an HSA to stay under the line, you're paying for everyday care yourself until the deductible. A direct primary care membership is one way some people cover that part for a flat fee, and in 2027 an HSA can pay a qualifying fee of up to $150 a month for one person.<sup><a href="#src-8">8</a></sup> The membership only covers that practice's own clinicians, so unless your current doctor is part of it, joining means switching.
