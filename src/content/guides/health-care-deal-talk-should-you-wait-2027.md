For the first time in a while, both sides in Washington are saying the same word about health care: maybe. **CBS News**' Kaia Hubbard reported in [Hakeem Jeffries outlines healthcare areas where Democrats "can explore coming together" with Trump](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/hakeem-jeffries-trump-democrats-healthcare/) that the incoming House Speaker answered the President directly. "Let's see what's possible in terms of what the president has said with respect to healthcare," Jeffries said on Face the Nation. Trump had told reporters, "I'm open to great healthcare," adding, "Now, can it be what they want? No. But it can be a combination of what we all want, and we could end up with great healthcare in our country."

Republicans are feeling the pressure from voters too. **Reuters**' Richard Cowan wrote in [Democrats won big in the midterms. So now what?](https://1027wbow.com/2026/11/21/democrats-won-big-in-the-midterms-so-now-what/) that Sen. John Boozman, an Arkansas Republican, said, "I think there's a real understanding in Congress that things like healthcare, the really exorbitant premiums, you know, that people are having to pay" must be addressed.

That's encouraging talk. But if you're shopping for 2027 coverage right now, here's what it does and doesn't change.

## What's on the table

- **What Democrats say they want first.** Jeffries has pledged to restore the expired ACA subsidies and reverse last year's Medicaid cuts, Time reports. On Face the Nation he also named lowering the Medicare age and adding vision, hearing and dental coverage as areas to explore.
- **What the White House has proposed.** The President's "Great Healthcare Plan" from January would "send money directly to the American people" rather than to insurers. It would also fund a cost-sharing reduction program that, the White House says, citing the Congressional Budget Office, would "reduce the most common Obamacare plan premiums by over 10%." Other pieces include plain-English insurer disclosures and requiring providers who take Medicare or Medicaid to "prominently post their pricing and fees."<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>
- **What's not settled.** There's no bill and no agreement on how far to go or how to pay for it. Democrats will be four votes short of the 60 most Senate bills need, Reuters reports.

## Why the stakes are real for your budget

The enhanced ACA tax credits ended after 2025. KFF found that people who stayed in the same plan saw their premium payments rise "by 114% or more than $1,000 annually." Because many people switched to cheaper plans, payments across all enrollees rose 58% on average, and deductibles rose 37%. Enrollment fell from 21.8 million to 19.2 million. For 2027, insurers asked for a median premium increase of about 15%.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

Bringing the credits back would be expensive. In an older estimate, CBO said making the expanded credits permanent would add $350 billion to deficits from 2026 to 2035 and cover 3.8 million more people by 2035.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>

## Should you wait? No.

Open enrollment runs from November 1 to January 15.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> To have coverage on January 1, pick a plan by December 15. Even if Congress acts quickly next year, the prices and help you're offered for 2027 are set by today's rules. Going uninsured while you wait leaves you exposed to the full cost of an accident or illness.

## Where direct primary care fits

If a lower-premium plan with a higher deductible is what you can afford for 2027, a direct primary care membership can cover everyday visits for a flat monthly fee while the plan stays in place for big bills. It isn't insurance, and it covers only the practice's own clinicians. [DPC plus a bronze plan, with worked numbers](/blog/dpc-plus-bronze-worked-numbers/) shows how the math can work.
