Lower health costs were a big theme of this election, and now the question is what the next Congress can actually do. **NBC News**'s Berkeley Lovelace Jr. laid out the options on November 4 in [5 healthcare fights Democrats could take on after midterms sweep](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/5-healthcare-fights-democrats-could-000255366.html). One idea is to restore the expired extra ACA tax credits and make them retroactive to early 2027. KFF's Larry Levitt told NBC, "That could both increase enrollment and provide cost relief to current ACA enrollees."

**The Washington Post**'s Rachel Roubein reported the same day that [Democrats pledge to quickly reverse changes to Medicaid, restore ACA subsidies](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/democrats-pledge-quickly-reverse-changes-183201572.html). She also spelled out the hurdles: "Yet passing legislation generally needs 60 votes in the Senate," unless lawmakers use a budget process, and President Trump has said he would probably become "Mr. Veto."

So there's real talk of relief, and real obstacles. Here's what that means for the plan you pick this month.

## What the new Congress says it wants

USA TODAY reports that restoring the extra ACA tax credits is expected to be high on the list, along with reversing the 2025 Medicaid cuts. Some Democrats also want to revisit bigger ideas like Medicare for All, though USA TODAY notes that in KFF polling, support drops when people hear about trade-offs like higher taxes. A KFF poll this spring found 64% of adults worry about affording health care.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup>

The stakes are large. KFF found that ACA marketplace insurers proposed a median premium increase of about 15% for 2027, which would be the second double-digit increase in a row.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> Based on CBO estimates, KFF says the 2025 law will add 10 million people to the uninsured by 2034, and more than 14 million once the expired tax credits are counted.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup><sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup>

## What stands in the way

Three things: the President's veto, the Senate's 60-vote rule for most bills, and cost. Levitt told USA TODAY he expects Trump would probably veto bills to restore the credits or reverse the Medicaid cuts. Timing matters too. A new Congress isn't sworn in until January, and open enrollment ends January 15 in most states.

None of this means nothing will happen. It means you can't count on it for your January bill.

## What to do now

- **Shop on today's prices.** Pick a plan by December 15 for January 1 coverage. If help arrives later, it would likely come as a lower bill or a refund, not as a reason you should have waited.
- **Keep your records.** Save your plan confirmation and premium statements. If relief is ever made retroactive, you'll want proof of what you paid.
- **Report income changes.** If your income goes up and you don't update your application, you may owe tax credits back when you file.
- **Look at the whole picture.** If a higher deductible is the only plan you can afford, a direct primary care membership can cover everyday visits for a flat monthly fee. It isn't insurance, and it covers visits only with that practice's own clinicians, so you'd still keep a plan for hospital and specialist care.
