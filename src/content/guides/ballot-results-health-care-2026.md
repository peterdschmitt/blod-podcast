Some of Tuesday's biggest health decisions weren't about candidates at all. On October 27, **ABC News**'s Mary Kekatos laid out one of them in [Abortion is on the ballot in Missouri again. Here's what to know about Amendment 3](https://abcnews.com/Health/abortion-ballot-missouri-amendment-3/story?id=137443465). She explained the history this way: "Following the Supreme Court decision, Missouri enacted a trigger ban, prohibiting most abortions in the state." Voters reversed that in 2024, and Amendment 3 asked them whether to restrict most abortions again.

On Election Day morning, **CBS News**'s Ian McKinney listed the rest in [Here's what the U.S. is voting on today for the 2026 midterm elections](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/what-us-voting-on-november-2026-elections/). One was California's Proposition 40, a one-time 5% tax on billionaires that would send money to health care, food assistance and education.

The counting ran late into the night. Here's where things landed by Wednesday morning, and what each result means for coverage and care. We describe each measure by what it does, not by who backed it.

## South Dakota kept its Medicaid expansion

Amendment I would have repealed the Medicaid expansion requirement in South Dakota's constitution if the federal share of expansion funding fell below 90%. When AP called the race, 56% of votes were against it, with more than 80% counted.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup> South Dakota Searchlight reports that among the roughly 143,000 South Dakotans covered by Medicaid, about 31,000 are enrolled through the expansion.<sup><a href="#src-1">1</a></sup>

**What it means for you:** if you're one of those 31,000, the vote doesn't change your coverage. The federal work, school or community service rules that start next year for expansion enrollees still apply, so watch your renewal mail. KFF counts 41 states, including D.C., that have adopted expansion, and notes South Dakota and West Virginia had been considering "trigger" laws that would end it if federal funding drops.<sup><a href="#src-2">2</a></sup>

## Virginia added birth control and fertility care to its constitution

Virginia's Question 1 protects "the freedom to make personal decisions" about care including prenatal care, childbirth, postpartum care, birth control, abortion, miscarriage management and fertility care. It also lets the state restrict abortion in the third trimester, with exceptions.<sup><a href="#src-3">3</a></sup> AP reports it passed.

**What it means for you:** a constitutional right isn't the same as insurance coverage. Your plan's rules on birth control and fertility treatment still decide what you pay. We couldn't find a stated effective date, so check with the state before counting on a change.

## Missouri, Idaho and Nevada voted on abortion

AP reports that Missouri voters defeated an amendment that would have restricted most abortions, so the 2024 amendment stays in place. Idaho voters defeated a measure that would have created abortion rights in state law, so current Idaho law stays. Mountain West News Bureau reported Idaho's measure failed 55.4% to 44.6% when AP called it.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup> AP reports that Nevada approved an amendment creating a right to abortion until fetal viability. That count was still running overnight, so treat it as unofficial.<sup><a href="#src-5">5</a></sup>

## California's Prop 40 hadn't been decided

Prop 40 would put a one-time tax on billionaires, with most of the money meant to replace federal health funding cuts. CalMatters reported it was trailing, about 57% no, with 35% of votes counted.<sup><a href="#src-4">4</a></sup> If it fails, there's no new state money to backfill those cuts, but nothing about your current Covered California or Medi-Cal plan changes because of the vote.

## What didn't change on Tuesday

- **Marketplace prices and deadlines.** Pick a plan by December 15 for January 1 coverage. Open enrollment runs through January 15. Medicare enrollees can change plans through December 7.
- **Federal Medicaid rules for 2027.** No ballot measure changed the federal work requirements.
- **Your everyday care options.** A direct primary care membership isn't insurance and doesn't depend on these votes, but it covers visits only with that practice's own clinicians. You'll still want coverage for hospital and specialist care.
